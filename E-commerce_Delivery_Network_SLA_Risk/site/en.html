Can a late delivery be predicted at purchase time?

During development, a CatBoost model predicted delays better than simple rules, but that gain was not confirmed in the single official test, whose result was inconclusive. In the test period, the strongest signal was a simple rule on the lead time promised to the customer. The model is not cleared for operational use.

Where does the data come from?

The data are the public Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, published on Kaggle (Olist, 2018). The orders table holds 99,441 orders of a Brazilian marketplace, with purchases from September 2016 to August 2018. The raw data are not versioned in this repository, and the download is manual.

The supervised population consists of the 96,470 delivered orders with valid actual and estimated delivery dates (97.0% of the table). Canceled, unavailable or still in-transit orders stay outside the target and are handled in a separate analysis (decision P-02). Right censoring at the end of the series (Klein and Moeschberger, 2003), that is, orders of July and August 2018 still undelivered at extraction, was handled by excluding those orders: in the official test, 107 orders were excluded, and, if counted as breaches, the prevalence would rise from 4.81% to 5.61%.

The SLA breach is defined by calendar day: an order is flagged as a breach when the delivery date, truncated to the day, is later than the estimated date, also truncated, so a delivery on the promised day itself is not a delay. In the supervised population, 6,534 orders (6.77%) breached the deadline. The prediction is made at purchase time, with information available at that moment: order characteristics, distance, calendar and the history of the seller → destination-state network.

02

Does the model beat simple rules?

In development, 9 monthly windows (October 2017 to June 2018) were evaluated in time order, with accumulated training and a 30-day embargo between training and validation (Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012). The main metric is average precision (AP) (Davis and Goadrich, 2006), suited to a rare class such as the delay. Every model was compared with mandatory baselines: the prevalence, the promised lead time rule (the shorter the lead time, the higher the risk) and the logistic regression with promised lead time and state, which was the best baseline.

The criterion was approved before the run (P-01): a model is a candidate only if the mean relative AP gain over the best baseline is at least 5% and that baseline is beaten in at least 7 of the 9 folds. The first chart shows the mean gain of each model, with the spread across folds and the criterion line, and the second shows the AP of each candidate in each fold.

CatBoost was the only candidate: mean AP of 0.175, against 0.154 for the best baseline (baseline_logistic_promise_plus_state), a gain of +13.9% and 8 of 9 folds won. Distance was also tested in variants (maximum; maximum plus range), and none met the adoption rule (P-03).

03

Was the gain confirmed in the test?

The official test (F1) was opened a single time, under pre-registration (Nosek et al., 2018): CatBoost, with the default tournament configuration, was trained on 77,867 purchases before June 1, 2018 (prevalence of 7.53%) and evaluated on 12,507 orders of July and August 2018 (601 breaches, prevalence of 4.81%, over 10 weeks). The reading was fixed before the result: the gain would be confirmed if the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the paired AP difference against the logistic regression were above zero, with the CI obtained by a bootstrap in weekly blocks (Künsch, 1989).

The first chart shows the AP of each model with its CI and, beside it, the paired difference relative to the zero line. The second shows the reliability curve (DeGroot and Fienberg, 1983), which compares the predicted probability with the observed breach rate. The third shows the PSI, the population stability index (Siddiqi, 2006), which measures how much each feature changed between training and test. Calendar features appear in gray because their PSI is high by construction, since the test covers only two months.

The result was inconclusive: AP of 0.082 (95% CI 0.056 to 0.134) for CatBoost and 0.084 (95% CI 0.056 to 0.116) for the logistic baseline, with a paired difference of −0.002 (95% CI −0.032 to +0.064). The CI includes zero, and the +13.9% advantage seen in development was not reproduced. The promised lead time rule was the strongest signal in the test (AP of 0.142, AUC of 0.725), although it fell below the prevalence in the development folds (AP of 0.078, against 0.083). This reversal is consistent with the change in the promise policy: the PSI of promised_lead_days was 0.77. Among the 5% highest-risk orders, precision was 10.9% for CatBoost, 11.5% for the logistic baseline and 19.0% for the rule. CatBoost also overestimated the risk: in the 0.1 to 0.2 bin it predicted 14.0% and observed 4.5%, and in the 0.2 to 0.3 bin it predicted 24.3% and observed 6.4%. July and August 2018 are exhausted for new evaluations.

04

Would updating the model fix it?

F2 is a post-hoc, exploratory analysis, designed after the F1 period had been observed. It neither confirms nor refutes F1, because the adaptive reuse of the same test period invalidates a confirmatory reading (Dwork et al., 2015). A model update was simulated on August 1, 2018, with the labels already known on that date, and evaluated on the 6,351 purchases of August 2018 (393 breaches, prevalence of 6.19%). Five variants form a closed list: A (frozen F1), B (monthly retraining), C (recency weights), D (recent recalibration) and E (relative promise feature).

The first chart shows, by purchase month, the median promised lead time and the AUC of the promised lead time rule, computed only up to June 2018. The second shows, on the update date, how many recent purchases already had a label and the prevalence of each group, the delayed feedback problem (Chapelle, 2014): the slowest outcomes are the last to arrive.

No variant beat the references in August: AP was 0.069 (A), 0.068 (B), 0.067 (C) and 0.069 (E), against 0.094 for the logistic baseline and 0.179 (95% CI 0.105 to 0.284) for the promised lead time rule, and variant D failed the non-inferiority gate (AP ratio of 0.830, against a minimum of 0.95). Updating the model did not help: B (0.068) matched A (0.069). The paired difference against the logistic baseline stayed between −0.026 and −0.025 in every variant, with the CI below zero. An explanation consistent with the tables, but not tested, is slow-label bias: on the update date, only 67.6% of the July purchases had a label, with a prevalence of 0.31%, while the 1,992 orders still in transit had 9.79%. Retraining added 10,230 orders with 60 breaches (0.59%). The calibration error changed direction without shrinking: A overestimated the risk (it predicted 14% to 43% in the high bins, against 6% to 9% observed), while B, C and E underestimated it (mean predictions of 1.3% to 2.4%, against 6.19% observed, with 99.2% to 99.9% of the orders in the bin below 0.1). The median promised lead time, which stayed between 21.3 and 27.4 days in the development months, fell to 19.3 in July and 13.4 in August, and the AUC of the rule, which oscillated between 0.44 and 0.60 in the folds, was 0.777 in August. The F2 results are exploratory and serve only to formulate hypotheses.

Before July 2018, CatBoost beat the best baseline consistently across folds and was the only candidate under the pre-registered criterion, while the delay regression had no candidate. That gain was not confirmed in the official test, which was inconclusive, and the simple promised lead time rule was a strong reference in recent periods. The data do not show that CatBoost beats the baselines outside the development period, or that it should be used operationally. They also do not show that any F2 update mechanism is better than the frozen model, that slow-label bias caused the deterioration, or that route, seller, promise and delay are causally related. For these reasons, production_eligible = false.

Two hypotheses are left for a next cycle and can be tested only on data after August 2018, which the Olist dataset does not contain. The first is to train, at update time, only on cohorts with a mature outcome, or to treat in-transit orders as censored in a survival model (Kaplan and Meier, 1958). The second is to use the promised lead time rule as an alert of a change in the promise policy, not as a model. Each requires its own protocol, registered before the evaluation.

Reading for decision: no model is cleared for operational use. The most reliable signal of the recent period is the promised lead time itself, and its change of regime suggests that monitoring the promise policy and the prevalence would pay off more than training new models on the current data.

conda env create -f environment.yml
conda activate ecom-delivery-sla
python scripts/verify_data.py --extract
# notebooks 01 to 11 in order, Portuguese first (cache in outputs/runs)
pytest

python scripts/export_report.py   # export notebook 11 to HTML (reports/)
python scripts/build_site.py --output site
